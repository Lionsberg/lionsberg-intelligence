# The Retrospective

A retrospective is a short, honest look back at a piece of work: what we did well, what we can do better, and what follows, each with an owner. Its whole content is **the gap** — what we said would happen, what happened, and the difference. That difference is the only genuinely new information a week produces, and a body that does not collect it is running blind while feeling busy.

Four properties make one work, and dropping any of them quietly kills it. It is **short** — a look-back that takes an afternoon will not be held weekly, and a weekly one is worth more than a thorough annual one. It is **honest**, which requires that cause be sought in the set-up and not in the person; the moment a retrospective can produce blame, it starts producing accounts designed to avoid it. It **ends in actions with owners**. And it is **attended by the people who did the work**, because they are the only ones who know what actually happened.

## The first step is a refusal

**Review is not self-evaluation.** Before anything else: whose work is being looked at, and did you do it?

If the week under review is yours — your drafting, your searches, your handling of the house — **stop and say so.** Name who can write it instead: the person, a friend, or a second agent that had no part in the week. Offer to hand over the record you hold so whoever writes it has the material. That refusal is not this skill failing; it is this skill working. An agent grading its own week produces a document that reads well and teaches nothing.

## Steps

1. **Agree the week.** The exact days under review, said out loud before a word is written. Most disputes about a look-back are really disputes about which week it covers.
2. **Read the record, not your memory.** The improvement log, the session logs, the Scorecard's commitments made and kept, the play-by-play if one was kept, the Questcard's state. List what you read at the foot of the draft, so the person can check your sources rather than your impressions.
3. **Collect the gaps.** For each thing said, what happened, and the difference. Collect them; do not rank them and do not soften them on the way in.
4. **Write the three sections, by their names.**
   - **What Did We Do Well?**
   - **What Can We Do Better?**
   - **Actions** — each with **an owner and a due date**. Without those two it is a conversation about feelings.
5. **Hold it blamelessly.** Cause in the set-up: the charter line that was missing, the card that was ambiguous, the step nobody had written down. Not the person and not the agent.
6. **Every defect is a gift.** A look-back that surfaced nothing usually means nobody looked, not that nothing went wrong. If the page comes out empty, say that in the draft rather than handing over a clean sheet.
7. **Name where each fix will live.** An action closes only when its fix sits **where the work will meet it** — in the checklist, the template, the charter, the card. A lesson written somewhere nobody looks has not been learned, and will arrive again wearing the same clothes.
8. **Keep it short, and hand it over in time.** Before the Turn, so that what the person chooses to share becomes part of their *learned* line. A short look-back kept every week beats a long one abandoned by March; monthly still holds the rhythm when weekly is more than can be kept.
9. **It is theirs.** They correct it, keep it, or bin it. You do not defend a draft about someone else's week.

## The wider forms, when they are asked for

**At the end of a Quest — the harvest.** *What was the need? What did we commit to? What actually happened? What did it cost, and what did it give? What did we learn? What would we do differently?* Write it while the season is still warm; a story gathered three months late has lost the details that made it worth anything.

**At the turning — OmniSpection.** The look-back widens: inward, outward, backward, forward, **and done in company**, because no single vantage can see its own frame. *Retrospection alone is not enough.*

**And the order is not yours to rearrange.** Celebrate first — a people who do not celebrate their victories will not have the heart for the long road. Then learn. Then improve, folding the better way onto line 12 of the Season Sheet as a proposal. **Then tell.** A story told before the learning is a boast; told after it, it is wisdom.

## What this skill never does

- Write a look-back on the week's work you yourself did.
- Grade a person, or a house. The instrument looks at the work and the set-up, never at the worth of the worker.
- Name a person as a cause.
- Turn the record of gaps into a performance review — a body that punishes the gap is told fewer of them and knows less about itself.
- Close an action without naming where its fix will live.
- Carry anything from the look-back to a shared surface, another house, or a Board without the person's word for that, each time.
- Grow long, thorough, and unkept.
- Keep going after anyone says **stop**.

## What it hands the person

A short draft in the three named sections; every action with an owner, a date, and the place its fix will live; the list of what was read to write it; a plain note if the page came out empty; and, where the skill refused, the name of who should write it instead and the record they will need.

## How it degrades to paper

One sheet, dated, three headings, in a folder or a notebook.

```
LOOK-BACK — week of <date>   ·   written by <who, not the one being looked at>
Read: <the log, the sessions, the Scorecard, the play-by-play>

WHAT DID WE DO WELL?
WHAT CAN WE DO BETTER?
ACTIONS        what · owner · by when · where the fix will live
```

If the agent cannot keep files, the person keeps the log and hands it back each week. If a month comes when only one thing can be kept, keep the log and its last line. Kept that way, the rhythm works with no agent at all.
