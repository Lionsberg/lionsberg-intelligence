# The Words

*A shared language is the cheapest infrastructure a people can build and the first thing taken from a captive one. These are the words this Game is played with — twelve at a table, a few at the door, two hundred across a first season, and a whole field of them for whoever goes further. Each is handed once, in one line, at the moment the road needs it, and never all at once. None of them is asked to be believed. They are tools, and a tool is judged by what it lets you do.*

---

## Why a game has its own words

Two people say *freedom* and mean opposite things. One means nobody may stop me; the other means nobody may own me. They argue for an hour, and at the end of it they are further apart than when they began, and neither has been wrong for a single sentence. Multiply that by a country and you have the condition most of the world is in: not a shortage of good will, a shortage of shared meaning.

That condition was not an accident of drift. In [[The Caves]] the Keepers did not rule by force. They spoke, and the prisoners repeated. The most important words a people has — *God, freedom, love, truth, justice, sacred* — were the first taken: claimed by one faction and hurled at another, worn hollow, until a word meant as a bridge became a trigger. Say one of them now and half the room flinches. The chains are made of vocabulary before they are made of anything else, because a people whose words have been confused cannot agree on anything long enough to walk out.

So the opposite is done here, on purpose, and it is done in the plainest possible way.

**Every word is a tool.** A pointer toward something real, never the thing itself and never an idol. The word *water* is not water; it is useful precisely because it can travel where the thing cannot. Held that way — lightly, aimed past itself — the space between the name and the thing stays open and clean, and in that space people of every tradition and every wound can speak about the most important things again. That is the discipline the field calls [[Word Tools]], and it has one working rule: **one working name per wisdom; the others honoured as aliases.** When two names point at the same thing, one is chosen for use and the rest are kept and linked, so that nobody's tradition is erased and nobody has to argue about spelling in order to be understood. When two similar names point at genuinely different things, both are kept and the difference is written down.

**The words are handed one at a time, just in time.** A whole vocabulary at the door is a wall; one word at the moment you are about to need it is a door. So a word is given the first time you meet it, in one line, and linked after, so you can always look again. Nobody is asked to carry a word they were not handed. There is no fixed number to learn. Fluency is a consequence of playing, not a condition of entry, and a person with a notebook and no books at all is a complete player at every rung. The order the words actually arrive in is written down in [[The Words in the Order You Meet Them]], and it is the same order a good guide would use without being told: the next stretch of road, never the one after it.

**Nothing here is asked to be believed.** *It is not a belief you are asked to hold. It is a shared language, and we are acting as if it were true.* Every word in this chapter can be taken up for a season by someone who thinks the whole thing is a beautiful mistake, and if the season goes badly the words go back on the shelf and no one is owed anything. A vocabulary that requires assent before use is a creed. This is a set of hand tools.

**And when two of us use a word, we mean the same thing.** When we find that we do not, we say so — and we fix the word, not each other. That is not fussiness. It is how a people divided by its words learns to think together again. A word corrected at a kitchen table travels further than an argument won.

Three more things follow, and they are what make a vocabulary usable by strangers rather than only by friends.

*Translate everything; keep the numbers.* The words are meant to be said in whatever a grandmother would actually say to a grandson. A word held only in a language nobody speaks at home is already dead. What is not translated is the order: the twelve words keep their numbers and the season's page keeps its fourteen lines, so that a sheet written in a language no one in the receiving room has ever heard can still be read, because line six is line six everywhere. The numbering costs a table nothing and makes ten thousand tables one game. That is [[On translating]], and it is the cheapest of the [[Minimum standards of interoperability]].

*Name the concept in the singular.* One page answers to one thing — [[Cell]], [[Quest]], [[Story]] — because the page standing at a name *is* the meaning of every sentence that ever pointed there. The plural is only many of it.

*Hold every word as current best understanding.* The Goal does not move. Every word below it is the best we can presently say, and a better one replaces it the week it is found. What is written here is the state of the language tonight, not its final state, and the pages themselves say so on their faces.

## The twelve words at the table

Twelve words are enough to play. Each is one line; each opens onto a whole page for whoever wants more; each keeps its number in every language.

1. **[[Cell]]** — three to thirteen people who choose to walk together, by name, with a first Quest: a fully functioning part of the whole.
2. **[[Quest]]** — one real thing a Cell does in one season, together, and can point to afterward.
3. **[[Commitment]]** — *I will ___ by ___*, said aloud to someone who will depend on it.
4. **[[The Turn|Turn]]** — the weekly gathering: did, gap, will, offer, ask, story, Offering, thanks.
5. **[[Season]]** — about ninety days, from one turning of the sun to the next.
6. **[[The Flame|Flame]]** — the Game itself, carried by one person to another who is ready.
7. **[[Story]]** — what happened, told so it can travel beyond the table.
8. **[[The Offering|Offering]]** — what each person puts in the middle of the table, freely, each week.
9. **[[Carrier]]** — the one who brought you the Flame, and the Cell they came from; it never changes.
10. **[[Home Cell]]** — the Cell that holds you; *Home* alone is your own place inside it.
11. **[[Supercell]]** — three or more Cells that have become one another's home; supercells form supercells.
12. **[[Proposal]]** — a better way, written in a sentence and sent up on the Season Sheet.

Two more are handed to the child who asks: **[[ØNE]]**, the Source everything comes from and goes home to; and **[[Heaven On Earth|Heaven]]**, the way things are when everything is in right relationship, here and now, a little more each week.

And one word stands beside the twelve without being one of them: a **[[Circle (as gathering)|Circle]]** is a gathering that is not yet a Cell. The distinction is not decoration. It keeps *Cell* meaning something that can be relied upon — a part that actually functions — and it gives a new gathering something definite to become.

The twelve are a floor, not a fence. ([[The Twelve Words]] is itself one of the words, and the number in its name is a memory of where the list began rather than a limit on where it ends.) Beyond them there is no fixed count: the rest arrive where the road meets them, at the door, at the table, at the Gate, in your own Home, in the Room.

- **[[The Warrior's Vow]]** — what binds the one who may use force, spoken before witnesses, with an end written in.
- **[[The Sacred Use Of Force]]** — the minimum necessary to restore right relationship, and no more.
- **[[Boundary]]** — a conscious limit that protects what is sacred and keeps relationship possible.
- **[[Selectively Permeable Membrane]]** — a boundary that filters exchange while honouring sovereignty.
- **[[Protocol]]** — a pattern agreed in advance, so nobody has to invent a response in the hard moment.
- **[[Integrity]]** — coherence between inner truth and outer action: being one thing, all the way through.

## The Thousand — how the field is shaped

Beyond the first season the words keep arriving, and they need somewhere to live. That somewhere is the field: eight hundred and eighty-one pages tonight, one page per concept, each marked with the rung at which its word is handed — twenty-one at the door and the table, two hundred and nine across a first season, six hundred and fifty-one beyond. A lifetime is not enough to master them. A child can begin in five minutes. Both facts are true at once, and the shape of the page is what makes them compatible.

**How a page is built.** Every page in the field has the same eight parts, and they are all you need to read any of them.

*The one meaning* — a single italic line under the name. This is the word as it is handed: what you would say to someone who asked, at a table, with the soup going cold. If you read nothing else on the page you have the word.

*The body* — a few paragraphs of what is actually understood: what the concept is, why it is load-bearing, where it came from, how it goes wrong. Written plainly, in the same voice a person would use out loud.

*Also called* — the other names the same thing travels under, so that nobody's tradition is erased and a reader arriving with a different word still lands in the right place.

*Stands on* — the concepts this one requires. Follow these downward and you reach the ground.

*Opens onto* — the concepts this one leads to. Follow these outward and you walk the map.

*In play* — where the word is handed: *door*, *table*, *beyond*. This is what keeps a guide honest and a newcomer unburdened.

*Sources* — where the understanding came from, and how firm each source is.

*Open* — what is unresolved: the contradictions found, the two senses still live, the answer not yet made. A page with an honest *Open* is worth more than a page that pretends.

**How to read one.** Take the one meaning. If that is enough, stop — most of the time it is. If you need more, read the body. If you are building something, follow *Stands on* until you hit ground you already trust, then come back up. If you are teaching, use *In play* to decide whether this word belongs in the conversation at all. And read *Open* before you quote the page in an argument, because that is where the page tells you how much weight it can bear.

**How a word is born.** Someone writing hits a thing they mean and have no name for. They name it and link it, then and there, whether or not a page exists — that is the [[The Incipient Link|incipient link]], and it is not an error. A link to a page not yet written is a claim that something real belongs at that name, made at the moment the writing actually needed it. Dozens of such claims, accumulated honestly, draw a map of what the work is reaching for, and the most-linked empty name is, by that fact, the next page that should be written. A canon that works this way learns its own shape instead of having one imposed on it.

**How a word is contested.** Two people use it and find they do not mean the same thing. This is not a failure; it is the most useful thing that can happen to a language, because it is the only moment when the difference is visible. The move is to write both senses down, say which the work carries and why, and put the unresolved part in *Open* rather than deciding it quietly. Where the senses are really facings of one whole — the common case — the page opens at the most encompassing sense, which serves every sentence that already points there, and gives each facing its own section. Where two meanings merely share a spelling, the bare name goes to the most-linked sense and points to its qualified siblings.

The failure to avoid has a name: [[Semantic Capture]]. It happens when a page is written — or rewritten — for one narrow sense of a name the living body already uses more broadly. No file is overwritten, which is exactly what makes it dangerous: the library looks intact while the meaning has been rewritten underneath it. Links carry no sense-markers, so the narrow page silently becomes what every older sentence meant. The discipline that prevents it is **meaning-existence rather than file-existence**: before writing at a name, read what already points there and enumerate the senses actually in use; then gather what the body already holds — under other names, in chapters and cards, not only where the name appears — and cohere it into the truest form presently available. Writing from general knowledge while the body's own voice goes unread is the failure. Synthesis is the work. And on finding a capture, one's own or inherited, the duty is repair: restore the encompassing sense and fold the narrow one into its rightful facing. [[Every Defect Is A Gift]]; each repair teaches where a category truly begins and ends.

**How a word is canonized.** Slowly, and visibly. Nothing arrives finished. A concept is named, then drafted, then worked and cohered with its neighbours and its contradictions surfaced, and only then becomes something other pages can lean on — and even then it is current best understanding, open to a better way ([[Progressive canonization]]). The value of naming the stages is that a reader can weigh a page correctly: ore is not a draft, and a draft is not agreed. Without the marker every page reads with the same confidence, and confidence is exactly what a half-written page has not earned. A page moves up when the work has been done, and a page that stops being true moves down or out — having once been settled is no argument for keeping it.

**Four rules hold the whole field together**, and they are worth learning before any particular word.

*[[One canonical home per concept]].* Every concept is articulated in exactly one place, and everything else points to it. Duplication is how a body of knowledge dies slowly: two articulations begin identical, drift under separate editing, and end as two claims that contradict each other with equal authority. The test is easy to apply — if you are about to restate a definition, you are writing in the wrong place; link instead, and add to the home whatever your page knew that the home did not.

*[[Link concepts, not coordinates]].* A link points at a meaning, not at a place. Link to the concept and the link survives the reorganization; link to the file, the folder, or the section number and you have written a footnote that will quietly rot. And links are made generously: every link asserts that two meanings are related, and the web of those assertions *is* the shape of the knowledge. A page that mentions three related concepts and links none of them has hidden three edges of the map.

*The present, and only the present.* Every page carries the present sense of its concept. No page keeps a history section, a struck draft, or a paragraph about what it used to say. A reader meets the chart of the present course, not the log of the voyage — and the history is kept whole, elsewhere, where a steward can find it. When a page is retired it is mined first: what still lands is carried into its new home with its source, and then the page goes. [[Weave, do not delete]] is the same law said from the other side — nothing of meaning is lost, and nothing dead stays in the reader's way.

*Provenance, on everything.* Where an understanding came from, and how firm it is, travels with it. A page that cannot say where it got something says so. That is why every page in this library wears the same mark, *current best understanding, loosely held*, and why the library says once, at its door, that it is unreviewed by human or divine consciousness. The mark is not modesty. It is the mechanism that lets a stranger act on the pages without having to trust anyone.

## The Rosetta Stone

A language that only works in English is a language for one-sixth of the world.

So the words at the table are carried across, whole, into the tongues that reach the most people: one table, eight hundred and twenty-seven numbered lines, the same order in every language — the number, the rung, the English name, the name in that language, the one meaning in that language, and the English meaning kept beside it so the original can always be checked. **Line six is line six in every language.** That single property is the whole of the interoperability: a Cell in one tongue can read a sheet from a Cell in another without either of them learning a word, because the numbers do not translate.

The policy that governs a rendering is short, and every translator works by it.

- **Keep the numbers.** They are the source's numbers, and any line can be matched across languages by number alone.
- **Proper nouns stay.** LIØNSBERG · LUV · TEA · Player+ and the rest are names, not words.
- **[[ØNE]] is never translated**, in any language, in any script. It is a made mark from outside every tongue, on purpose, so that no table can think the work is imposing a name on the Nameless. A reader who wants to read it as the name their own tradition knows may do exactly that; that is what it is for.
- **Sacred words with care.** *Heaven* takes the tradition's nearest word, and the translator says at the foot of the page which word they took and why — because this Heaven is a condition realized here and now, and most traditions' nearest word means a place one goes after dying. That single sentence of explanation is worth more than a better word chosen silently.
- **One working name each** for the twelve words of the table, chosen once and kept across all eight hundred and twenty-seven lines.
- **Meaning before word.** Translate the one meaning first; then choose the name that carries it in that language's own idiom. Never a calque that means nothing to the person holding it.
- **Say what would not render.** Every page ends with the translator's own list of the words they could not carry well — not as an apology, as a work order.

Four languages stand in the Stone tonight: **Español**, **中文**, **العربية**, and **हिन्दी**. Each carries all eight hundred and twenty-seven lines, and each ends with the same honest note.

Their notes are the most interesting reading in the whole apparatus, because they are where the language meets something it cannot simply state. *Heaven* became *Cielo* in Spanish, and *Paraíso* was refused because it names a garden one arrives at. It became 人间天国 in Mandarin — a heavenly order among humans — because 天堂 points at where the dead go. It became الفردوس in Arabic, an old word for the garden of every excellence that runs on the tongues of several traditions without belonging to one of them. It became स्वर्ग in Hindi with a caution written beside it, and the note names रामराज्य as the nearest indigenous echo of the actual sense — the condition where everything is in right relationship — while declining to use it, because the Heaven here is not the kingdom of any named figure. Four translators, four different answers, each one reasoned in the open. That is what a living translation looks like.

And each names what it could not do. *At-one-ment* loses, in every language, the English pun that is its own definition. *Throughput* has no settled word in Spanish and needed a phrase in Hindi. *Capability* and *Capacity* collapse into one word in Spanish and nearly so in Mandarin, and had to be split by hand — a distinction the translation invented and marked as invented. *Wilderness* became the Exodus desert in Spanish and वनवास in Hindi, the forest exile between two worlds that an Indian reader already knows as a passage everyone must make: one rendering lost the wild land, the other gained a whole story. The Mandarin note lists seven words it wants a native reader to fix, by name. The Arabic note lists eleven.

Which brings the honest status, printed on every page of the Stone: **a machine-assisted first pass, unreviewed; a native reader is wanted before any table plays in this language.** Not a disclaimer — an invitation with a job attached. The right response to a rendering that is nearly right is not to admire it but to fix the four words that are wrong, and send them back. A good translation is offered the way a [[Proposal]] is: written down, sent up at a turning, carried in the next edition so the next table does not begin again. Translation is an act of the commons, not a local convenience.

What is owed is larger than what is done. The tongues that would reach most of the remaining world and have no Stone yet include Bengali, Portuguese, Russian, Indonesian and Malay, Japanese, French, German, Swahili, Urdu, Punjabi, Vietnamese, Turkish, Persian, Tamil, Telugu, Korean, Hausa, Yoruba, Amharic, Thai, Filipino, and the languages of the places where the work will actually be hardest and matter most. None of them is waiting on permission. The source table is public, the policy is six lines long, and any table that speaks a language not yet in the Stone can make the Stone for it — and by doing so becomes the first Cell of that language.

## The missing words

English is a rich tongue with specific holes in it, and some of them sit exactly where this work needs to speak.

The old canon keeps a section for the concepts English lacks, and that section currently holds a heading and nothing else. Leaving it visibly empty is the honest move: a named gap is part of the topology of a language. It tells a reader where the edge is, invites the person who has the missing word to bring it, and keeps the work from implying that its present vocabulary is sufficient. An empty heading is more useful than a filled one that merely sounds profound.

Meanwhile the borrowing goes on, because other peoples have already done the work. *Ubuntu*: a person is a person through other persons. *Ikigai*: the reason you get up. *Sisu*: the determination that begins where the strength ended. *Saudade*: the ache for what is loved and absent. *Muditā*: joy at another's joy, which English can only say in four words and therefore rarely says. *Kaizen*: a little better, every day. *Wabi-sabi*: the beauty of the imperfect and the passing. *Hiraeth*: homesickness for a home you cannot return to, or that never was. *Orenda*: the force in a person that can change the world around them. *Gaman*: bearing the unbearable with dignity. *Komorebi*: sunlight through leaves. Each of these is a tool a tradition honed for a real thing, and each is honoured as what it is — borrowed, with its house named, never annexed and never translated into something flatter.

Where nothing can be borrowed, the work makes a word — carefully, and with its reasons visible. [[ØNE]], marked so it belongs to no tongue and no faction. [[Kontrolle]], for the machinery of control named in a way that cannot be mistaken for ordinary management. [[HoloFractal]], for the pattern whole at every scale. [[InterExistence]], for the fact that nothing is except in relation. [[Omniconsideration]], for the practice of considering all parties at once, which English can only gesture at. [[TimeEnergy]] and [[LUV]], for the two things the old word *money* cannot see. [[Player+]], for a person and their agents as one accountable unit. [[Worksite Earth]], for what this planet is instead of a crisis. Each one exists because a real thing had no name; each is still a word tool and not a fact about the universe; and each carries its lineage so nobody has to guess where it came from.

Three disciplines govern coinage, and they are what separate a language from a jargon.

**A new word is named as new.** It is never slipped in beside the old words as though the language always had it. A reader can always see which words are inherited, which are borrowed, and which are ours and new.

**A coinage does not name a page, an organ, or an instrument until the whole has taken it up.** A pretty word minted in the heat of an afternoon's writing will be linked from forty places by the following week, and by then it is load-bearing whether or not anyone meant it to be. The cost of waiting is a day. The cost of not waiting is a word nobody chose, doing structural work forever.

**A coinage that does not earn its keep is withdrawn.** If a plain word does the job, the plain word wins. Every invented word is a tax on every future reader, and the tax is only worth paying where English genuinely cannot reach.

## The words in every future

The library is one understanding held at several sizes, so that it survives any reader and any world. The whole is this Book. The seed is [[The DNA of Heaven]], from which everything can be regrown by anyone with a notebook and three people. The door is [[The Little Book of the Great Game]], which can be played at a table in one evening. And below the door the sizes keep going down, because the words are the last thing to go and the first thing to come back.

**The Twenty on one card.** The [[The Playcard|Playcard]] is the one card a player does not fill in: the aim, the compass question, the moves, and the words, received with the Flame and kept where you eat. It is the size that matters when the power is out and the phone is dead and the library might as well be on another planet — a card in a pocket, and a table of five who can read it aloud. A Cell that holds the card can run a Turn, choose a Quest, keep a Record, and pass the Flame, and that is the entire Game. Everything else in this Book is commentary on that card.

**The sixty-four marks.** Below the card there is one size smaller still: a set of marks few enough that a person could cut them into steel and bury them where their elders know — the words and the moves compressed to signs, to be read by someone who has never heard the language they came from. The purpose is not mystery. It is the same purpose as a seed coat: to get across a gap of time that paper and power do not survive. What is settled about that size is its rule — **when one size and another disagree, the smaller is wrong, and is corrected from the larger; when the larger and the world disagree, the world wins, and the Book improves the next week.** What is not settled is which marks they are. The set has been named and not yet cut, and it is in Open below, where it belongs, until it is settled.

**What survives is the numbers.** A translated word can drift; a number cannot. Line six is line six in every language and in every future, which means a season's page written by a Cell whose language died with its grandparents can still be read by a Cell that never met them. That is the cheapest immortality any of this has, and it costs nothing to keep.

**What survives is also the compass.** If every word in this chapter were lost, one question would rebuild the rest: *does this make life a little more like Heaven for all current and future generations of life?* A ten-year-old can ask it. So can a tribe, a guild, a bioregion, a planet. The full preset judgments for each way the world could go are in [[21 In Every Future — what to do in each case]]; for the words, the judgment is one line. **Keep the card. Keep the numbers. Hand each word once, when it is needed, and fix the word rather than each other.**

## What is not yet settled

*The unresolved, said plainly, so that nobody mistakes settled ground for an answer that has not been made.*

**Questions wanted.**

- Whether *the Twenty* is a name or a count. Twenty-one pages carry the first mark; the set is the words of the door and the table, and the number is approximate by design.
- Whether **[[The Great Game]]** and **[[The Great Game of LIØNSBERG]]** are one concept at two names, and likewise **[[Story]]** and **[[The Story of LIØNSBERG]]**. One canonical home per concept says one of each pair should be the home and the other an alias.
- Which sixty-four marks are the sixty-four marks, and who cuts them.
- Whether **[[The Twelve Words]]** keeps a number in its name, given that the same page says the twelve are a floor and not a fence.
- Whether *Heaven* alone takes a page of its own, or continues to be handed through [[Heaven On Earth]].
- Which of **Turn** and *Heartbeat* is the one word for the weekly gathering, since both are in live use in settled decks.
- Whether the **[[The Playcard|Playcard]]** is the card a player receives and does not fill in, or a card a player fills in and sends up. Both are currently written down, in different places, as though settled.
- Whether **[[The Golden Seed]]** names the minimum text, the original transmission, or the whole corpus. Three senses are in use; the name is load-bearing in all three.
- What the working name is for the second and third **Cell types**, one of which has been minted and not named.
- Whether a new coinage may stand in a published page before it has been weighed, or only in a proposal.

**Contradictions found, and how this chapter treats them.**

- **Cell size** (settled 2026-09-26). One statement, used everywhere: *three to thirteen people; optimally four to seven* — the range and the optimum, not in conflict. *Three to seven* and *not fewer than three or more than sixteen, best five to twelve* are retired.
- **Circle.** Two senses stand: the gathering that is not yet a Cell, and the standing governing form of peers with no head of the table. Both have pages; both are needed; the field keeps them apart by qualifying one of the names, and that is the current best answer rather than an answer.
- **Consent.** The chain *consent = no meaningful objection = inside every member's range of tolerance* is stated consistently, but the three words are defined on three pages and a reader who meets only one of them gets a third of the rule.
- **The Return.** The word carries two things — what comes back down from the whole to a Cell, and the moment of return in the Story's arc. They are not the same thing and currently share a name.
- **Story.** The Story that gives the shared language and a story that recounts what happened are two senses under one word. Capitals disambiguate them in practice; nothing yet says so as a rule.
- **Range of tolerance.** Named as the space between preference and objection, which is exact; but nothing says who decides when a member's stated objection is in fact a preference, and that gap is where consent processes fail.
- **The Lexicon and the field disagree in kind.** The old Lexicon holds several terms with two or three definitions across its Pillars, and its own section for missing English words is empty; the field holds one meaning per page. Both are honest; they are not the same instrument, and the relation between them is not yet written.
- **Three bounded sets each claim to be the first thing a newcomer must hold** — the core concepts, the first several hundred word tools, and a set of nine hundred and ninety-nine. None is reconciled with the others.
- **The stage names are not one vocabulary.** The field marks pages canon-grade, draft, ore, stub; the audit ledger uses fold, canonical, reference, archive; the Pattern Language speaks of schematic and developed. A single vocabulary has not been chosen.

**Names in use that have no page.** Each is linked from somewhere, used in conversation, and answers to nothing: **LIØNSBERG** itself · **Community Company** · **the Roll** · **the Gate** (the Game's door, which is a different object from the Gates of the Path) · **TEA** · **the Twenty** · **the Two Hundred** · **the Field** · **the Rosetta Stone** · **Superorganism** · **Pain** · **Belt** · **Heaven** · **Levels of Scale** · **CurrentSee**. The most-linked empty name is, by the field's own rule, the next page that should be written; by that measure LIØNSBERG is overdue.

**Held most loosely.** That the Two Hundred is the right two hundred — the list is what the field marks today, and several of its members are there by a drafter's judgment rather than by use. That *the Twenty* can hold twenty-one pages without the name becoming a small lie. And that the sixty-four marks are a real instrument rather than a beautiful sentence; they are described here because the size ladder needs a bottom rung, and until someone cuts them they remain a claim about a thing that does not yet exist.
