# Collective Bankruptcy

*The recognition that the claims outstanding cannot all be honoured — and the choice that follows: spend the next thousand years co-creating, or spend them fighting over who is owed what.*

A bankruptcy is not primarily a moral event. It is the moment a fiction stops being maintained: the claims on a body exceed anything it can produce, everyone has known for some time, and the pretending has become more expensive than the admitting. What this names is that condition at a scale where there is no court and no one outside to administer it ([[Money]], [[Usury]], [[Scarcity]], [[The Endgame Cascade]]).

The move it proposes is not repudiation and not repayment; both are versions of insisting the claims are real. It is **release** — the same move the Jubilee tradition makes, arrived at by arithmetic rather than by piety ([[Jubilee]], [[Forgiveness]], [[The Act Of Release]], [[Covenant]]).

The reason given is entirely practical and is the strongest sentence in the chapter. If the claims cannot be honoured, the only question left is how much is destroyed in the fighting over them — and the fighting over unpayable claims is what produces the wars that follow every monetary order's end ([[Pre-Collapse Proaction vs Post-Collapse Reaction]], [[Multi-Polar Traps]], [[The Asymmetry of Consequences]]).

It joins the other side of the same word. What cannot be paid is written off; what was inherited from everyone who came before is taken up and built on. Release of the unpayable, and stewardship of what was given — one motion, stated at both ends ([[Collective Inheritance]], [[Knowledge as commons]], [[Commons]]).

And the hardest part, which the chapter puts as a question rather than an answer: a release has to be genuinely better for the children of those who hold claims as well as for the children of those who owe. A jubilee that is a transfer is a defeat for someone and will be fought ([[Omniconsideration]], [[None Of Us Until All Of Us]], [[Reconciliation]], [[Navigating Difference]]).

**The chapter is written in the interrogative throughout** — it asks how, and does not answer. Its arithmetic lives in companion chapters, whose figures are those sources' own, dated, and estimates rather than measurements; none is carried here.
