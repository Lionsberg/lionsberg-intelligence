# Tragedy of the Commons

*The inherited argument that shared resources are inevitably depleted — a failure mode of ungoverned access, not of sharing, and the objection the Pattern answers.*

The received story: individuals acting in self-interest overuse a shared resource until it is destroyed, because each gains the whole benefit of their own use while the cost of overuse is spread across everyone. Popularized in a 1968 essay with the example of communal grazing land, it has been used ever since as the standing argument for privatizing anything held in common, on the reasoning that only an owner will look after a thing.

It is worth stating precisely because the precision is the refutation. The scenario describes *open access* — a resource with no membership, no agreements, no monitoring, and no consequence for abuse. Actual historical commons rarely looked like that. They had a defined body of commoners, rules the commoners made themselves, boundaries on use, someone watching, graduated consequences, and a way to settle disputes. Where those conditions held, commons lasted centuries; where they were dismantled, the resource collapsed — often *after* enclosure rather than before it. The tragedy is a tragedy of ungoverned access, and the cure is governance by the users, not ownership by one of them.

Which is why every element that makes a [[Commons]] work here is a direct answer to a clause of the story. [[Selectively Permeable Membrane]] gives the boundary: who is in, and on what terms. [[Field of Agreements]] gives the rules, made by the people bound by them. [[Subsidiarity]] puts the decisions at the scale that can see the resource. [[Transparency]] and [[Accountability]] supply the watching. [[Stewardship]] supplies the disposition, and [[The Parasite]] names, without euphemism, the person who shows up to drain a commons rather than build it.

The one-line version a Cell can use: a commons without a membrane is not a commons, it is a car park. Govern the access and the tragedy does not arrive.
