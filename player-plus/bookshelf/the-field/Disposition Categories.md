# Disposition Categories

*The small vocabulary an audit uses to say what becomes of each source: fold, canonical, reference, archive, or look again.*

Auditing a large corpus needs a decision per source, and decisions need a shared word each. **Fold** — this book's substance belongs inside a canonical volume, and the audit says which one. **Canonical** — this book *is* a volume, or its substantive heart. **Reference** — keep it as supporting material and link to it from the canonical home; it is commentary, not canon. **Archive** — preserve it with its provenance as part of the procession; it is not folded. And a mark for *this needs a deeper read before anyone decides*, which is the honest answer more often than the others.

The categories are small on purpose. A vocabulary of five can be applied consistently by many readers across hundreds of sources; a richer one produces disagreements about the labels instead of about the work. What makes them usable is that each carries an action: a fold is work someone must do, an archive is a move someone must make, a reference is a link someone must write.

They also make the judgment visible. A first-pass disposition based on a title and a quick scan is recorded as exactly that, and the deeper audit either confirms it or overturns it — in the open, where the reasoning can be argued with. An audit that produces only a cleaned-up result, with no record of what was decided about what, cannot be checked by anyone afterward.

Nothing in the vocabulary permits deletion. Archive preserves; fold weaves before it consolidates.
