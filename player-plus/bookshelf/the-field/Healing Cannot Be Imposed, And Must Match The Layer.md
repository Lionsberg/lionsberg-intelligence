# Healing Cannot Be Imposed, And Must Match The Layer

*Two rules for anyone who mends: it requires the other's consent — as a clause of the vow, not as advice — and it has to be applied at the layer the wound is actually on.*

**Consent, and it is enforceable.** Even healing — perhaps especially healing — cannot be imposed on the unwilling. What is offered must be received. Someone who heals without consent has violated the protection of the unwilling that their vow already binds them to, which moves this out of the register of good manners and into the register of things a body can hold someone to ([[Consent]], [[Mutual Consent]], [[The Warrior's Vow]]).

The hard case is named rather than dodged: the one who refuses keeps the right to refuse. The one who offered may grieve the refusal and does not override it. That is the whole test, and it is a real cost — which is exactly why it is written as a clause rather than left to the judgment of whoever is convinced they are helping ([[Sovereignty]], [[Dignity]]).

Three disciplines travel with it. Not from above: the one mending is not superior to the one wounded, and both are equally accountable and equally walking ([[Right Relationship]]). And release afterwards: what flowed through is not the mender's accomplishment, and claiming it is the beginning of a drift ([[Conduit]], [[The Act Of Release]]).

**Match the layer.** Read where the wound actually is and address it there. A wound of the body is met at the body; an emotional wound at the emotions; something carried at depth is met at depth. Applied at the wrong layer, care either simply fails or adds disturbance — the common experience of being treated attentively for the wrong thing ([[Sensing The Field]], [[Root Causes]]).

The parallel with consent is exact and worth holding: consent is withdrawn at the level it was given, and healing is offered at the level the wound lives on. Both are instances of one rule — work where the thing actually is, not where it is easiest to reach.
