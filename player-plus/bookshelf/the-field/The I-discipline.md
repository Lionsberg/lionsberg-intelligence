# The I-discipline

*The teller's* I *is rare and charged — summons-grade only. The curatorial* I *dissolves.*

Two first persons look identical on the page and do opposite things. The **charged I** is the narrator's own witness, spent at the moments a reader must not remain a spectator: *I have heard it four times. I intend to hear it here.* It costs something to say and it summons. The **curatorial I** is the scholar's voice leaking back into the telling — *I was told, I learned, I hold, I call her* — and every appearance of it reminds the reader that they are being shown a collection rather than a world ([[The Four Voices]], [[Storytelling]], [[The LIØNSBERG Voice]]).

The rule is that the second one dissolves: into the tellings themselves, into the worlds they came from, or into the *we* of those who carry them — *many of us have carried that question*. The plural deepens the mystery of who is speaking; the solitary curatorial I spends it ([[Elision]], [[Author's Voice Is Sovereign]], [[Attribution]]).

It is load-bearing because it is a discipline on the writer's ego in a place where ego is invisible to the writer. Nobody intends to insert themselves into a sacred telling. It happens through a hundred small honest phrases — the ones that show the work, mark the sourcing, and quietly convert a myth into a report about a myth ([[Be the canon, don't narrate it]], [[Semantic Capture]], [[Wise Right Speech]]).

Its companion is the honest bow. Where a claim is, the register to use is not the first person but the attribution: *the oldest tellings say.* That is honesty rather than voice — an epistemic gesture, distinct from the banned curatorial I, and it strengthens the telling exactly where a personal claim would weaken it ([[The Truth law (of a true myth)]], [[We Do Not Know]], [[Provenance]]).
