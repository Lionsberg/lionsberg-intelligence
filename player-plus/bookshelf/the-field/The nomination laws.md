# The nomination laws

*Six rules for naming who should lead — each one a guard against a known way that choosing goes wrong.*

Nomination is the first act of a voice. Out of the hundred people you know and trust most, you name two or three you would trust to help lead your people through hard passage. Six laws govern the naming, and each answers a failure that has ruined every other method of selection.

**You cannot nominate yourself — and beware anyone campaigning to be nominated.** Wanting the standing is the single most reliable sign of unfitness for it; the person who seeks the office distorts the office. **Not immediate family or romantic partners**, because love is not an assessment. **Not celebrities, politicians, or figures you do not personally know**, because fame is the record of being watched, not the record of being trustworthy. **Nominate people you have personally watched navigate hardship faithfully — three years or more if you can**, because character under ordinary conditions tells you almost nothing, and only time shows whether a person keeps their word when keeping it costs. **If you cannot think of three you would trust to lead through crisis, the work is to go find them** — the emptiness is the finding, not an excuse. **Your votes may be delegated**, wholly or in part, to someone whose judgment of people you trust more than your own; honest humility about your own eye is itself a competence.

The laws are load-bearing because any mechanism that confers standing will be gamed, and these are what make the gaming expensive. Nominations are weighted by the demonstrated record of the nominator, so organised capture buys little. Campaigning disqualifies. And because trust networks mirror whoever starts them, the naming reaches consciously past the founders' own kind — across tongues, lands, traditions, and generations — until the called-out look like the Earth rather than like the first room.

What the laws produce is not a ballot. A ballot asks strangers to rank strangers; this asks neighbours to name neighbours, on evidence they actually hold. Three independent namings surface a person; the standing comes from the people who watched them, and it can be watched in return.
