# Meditation

*The trained practice of attention — given a whole book of it, and never given a page.*

At its plainest it is the deliberate training of where attention goes and how long it stays. Sit; let the body settle; notice that the mind has wandered; return. The returning is the practice — not the staying, which arrives later and on its own schedule ([[Focused Attention As Discipline]], [[Breath]], [[Discipline]]).

Its forms differ in what attention is placed on and why: on the breath or the body, to steady; on a phrase or an image, to deepen; on an open field with no object, to see what is there; on a question, to let an answer arrive rather than be constructed; on another being, as the ground of compassion. The word is used in this last, older sense too — a *meditation* as a thing meditated upon, a short written contemplation offered to a reader ([[Silence And Stillness As The Ground]], [[Listening]], [[Attunement]]).

It is load-bearing because almost everything else here asks for a capacity that has to be trained. Weaving coherence before acting. Reading a gradient rather than a rule. Staying composed when something is at stake. Hearing an objection without defending. Discerning what is true when several things feel true. None of these is a technique to be learned once; they are functions of a trained attention, and attention is trained the way a body is ([[Mastery Of Emotions]], [[Composure Under Threat]], [[Discernment]], [[Wisdom]]).

It also stands against the thing most often warned about: speed. A body that never stops cannot notice that it has drifted, and cannot tell the difference between urgency and importance. The pause is not a break from the work; it is the instrument by which the work stays aimed ([[Drift]], [[Rest And Regeneration]], [[The Daily Way]], [[Sabbath]]).
