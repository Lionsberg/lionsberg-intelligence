# Constitutional Protection Of Purpose

*Purpose embedded in the governance architecture itself, so that it cannot be amended away, voted out, or bought — together with the asset and anti-enclosure locks that make the protection real.*

A mission statement is a promise about intentions; a constitutional protection is a structure that makes betraying them impossible without first dismantling the structure in the open. The distinction is the whole point. Good intentions do not replicate; structures do.

It is load-bearing because mission drift is one of the predictable diseases, and it is the one that never feels like a betrayal from the inside. A body serves its purpose, then serves its own continuation, then serves whoever funds its continuation — each step defensible, the destination unrecognizable. Purpose made structurally sovereign means the drift has to break something visible before it can proceed.

Its instruments are concrete and borrowed from working practice. **Asset locks**: accumulated assets cannot be distributed to private individuals; they stay in the commons for the ongoing purpose, and nobody extracts more than what they put in plus honest pay for honest work. **Anti-enclosure mechanisms**: no group may privatize the commons. **Capped investor rights**: capital has a voice and never a veto ([[Capital Subordinate To Purpose]]). And a high bar for constitutional amendment, so that the protection is not removable by an ordinary majority on an ordinary day.

In play, a [[Cell]] with no assets meets none of this. A body that acquires money, land, tools, or a name worth something meets all of it at once — and the canon's counsel is to set the locks before there is anything to lock, because afterwards the conversation is about interests.
