# Probability, Severity, and Scope

*The three-variable frame every serious risk discipline uses: how likely, how bad, how wide. One extreme warrants attention; two demand mobilisation; all three at once leaves one rational response.*

It is the working frame of insurers, engineers, clinicians, emergency planners and military staffs, and it is worth having because it converts a feeling into a decision. **Probability** — how likely is this. **Severity** — how bad if it happens. **Scope** — how many people and systems are inside it ([[Decision Making]], [[Measurement]], [[Advantage Based Decision Making]]).

The escalation rule is the instrument. Any one extreme is a matter for attention. Two extremes together demand immediate mobilisation. Three simultaneously leaves nothing to weigh, because the ordinary reasons for waiting — cost, uncertainty, inconvenience — are all outranked by two of the three variables at once ([[Critically Effective Time Limit]], [[The Window]], [[Prioritizing]]).

What makes it more honest than argument is that it is **applied domain by domain rather than asserted whole**. Each strand is assessed on its own terms; the frame's claim is only that they must then be stacked *together*, because they are not independent — the failure of one raises the probability of failure in the others ([[Multi-Polar Traps]], [[Systemic Failure Modes]], [[The Endgame Cascade]]).

Its scope argument is the one most easily missed, and it is structural rather than rhetorical: earlier civilisational failures were regional because there were places outside the system. In an arrangement where every person is inside a single interlocked set of financial, logistical, informational and ecological systems, there is no outside — which changes scope from a variable into a constant ([[The Bronze Age Parallel]], [[The Planet]], [[Supply Chains]]).

It also runs down. A Circle deciding under uncertainty, a household deciding about a dependency — the same three questions, the same escalation rule ([[One tool, every scale]], [[The Probability Calculation Method]], [[The Asymmetry of Consequences]]).

Its source assigns each variable an extreme value for the present hour, citing published assessments and its own reading. Those are the source's judgments, from 2026, and are estimates rather than measurements; the frame is what crosses.
