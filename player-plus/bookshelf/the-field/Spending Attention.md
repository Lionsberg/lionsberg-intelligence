# Spending Attention

*Attention, not time, is the ultimate scarce resource — and what you spend it on is perhaps the most important moral decision you make.*

Attending to anything is a sacrifice, in the literal sense: to attend to this is to forgo attending to everything else, in this moment, permanently. There is a cost to every act of perception, and it is not recoverable. The chapter's move is to take that seriously as an economy — attention has a budget, it is spent continuously whether or not anyone is choosing, and it is the scarcest input a person has.

The comparison with time is where the claim sharpens. Time, the chapter says, is fungible and in some sense illusory; your attention is neither. Two people given the same hour do not receive the same thing, because what an hour contains depends entirely on what was attended to within it. So the practical question is never *do I have time*, which is usually a question about ordering, but *what am I spending myself on* ([[Attend To The Most Important Things First]], [[Spending Time · Units of Time]], [[TimeEnergy]]).

Calling it a *moral* decision is the deliberate part, and it follows from what attention does. Time, energy, resources and co-creation all follow attention; what is attended to receives a life's actual weight, and what is unattended effectively does not exist for the one not attending. A person's real values are therefore not what they profess but what their attention has been spent on, and that is legible in a way professions are not ([[Values]], [[Perception]], [[Focused Attention As Discipline]]).

Then the chapter runs a chain down to the ground. You can only attend to what you can perceive. You can only perceive in accordance with your [[Structure of Value]]. That structure is incoherent unless united by a single highest value; and your goals are incoherent unless united by a single highest goal ([[The three nested holarchies — Issues, Opportunities, Goals]]). Therefore, by definition, both can only be [[ØNE]] — or the structure of your reality lacks [[Integrity]] and comes apart ([[One (the One Thing)]], [[Coherence]]).

Two things that follow are worth saying plainly. Attention deliberately captured by systems built to fragment it is not a personal weakness but an expropriation of the scarcest thing a person owns ([[Technology Is Not Neutral]], [[Surveillance]]). And the capacity to place, hold and release attention is trainable, which is where this counsel stops being a judgment and becomes a practice ([[Focused Attention As Discipline]], [[Silence And Stillness As The Ground]]).
