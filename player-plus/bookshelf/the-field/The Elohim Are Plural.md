# The Elohim Are Plural

*A reading of the Hebrew scriptures: that a grammatically plural word was rendered by a singular one, and that restoring the plural changes what the text is about.*

The philological observation is not exotic and is not the corpus's invention. *Elohim* is plural in form; Hebrew has singular words available — *El*, *Eloah* — and does not use them in those places; several passages carry plural verbs and plural pronouns with it: *let us make*, *like one of us*, *let us go down*. Scholars have read this many ways for a long time: as a plural of majesty, as a survival of an older council of gods, as a title that shifted meaning across centuries of composition. The corpus takes the second road and takes it far — reading the scriptures as a witness to a populated field of powers, factional and territorial, within which one is allotted one people.

What the corpus does with it is where care is owed. It claims the singular rendering is not an accident but a consolidation, useful to institutions that needed one god to authorise one hierarchy; and in a companion Card it goes further, separating the deity of the Hebrew scriptures from the Source. That second step is the sharpest theological claim in the whole corpus, it bears directly on what living peoples hold sacred, and it is not settled here. The discipline that applies is the one this corpus applies to any inherited text: reconstruction dates, the pathway a reading travelled, and the limits of consensus, stated before the conclusion ([[Navigating Towards Truth]], [[The Old Authorities]], [[Scripture]]).

This is claim and reading, not knowledge — and it is claim about someone else's scripture, which raises the standard rather than lowering it. [[We Do Not Know]] applies. Nothing in the Game requires it; no Cell should be asked to hold it; and the corpus's own stated posture is that the restoration is offered to deepen a reader's relation to the Source, never to attack a reader's faith ([[Reverence]], [[Welcoming The Stranger]], [[ReHarvesting the Great Traditions]]).

The durable residue is a habit any serious reader can keep: when the English says *God*, ask which underlying word is there. That much is scholarship, and it costs no one their tradition.
