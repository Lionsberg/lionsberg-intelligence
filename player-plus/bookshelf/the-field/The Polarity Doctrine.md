# The Polarity Doctrine

*The canon has written the light pole of nearly every polarity and left the dark pole blank — while the compass question needs both.*

Heaven has a page and Hell does not. Good, and no Evil. Life, and no Death. Light, and no Darkness. Justice, and no Injustice. The True Self, and no false self. Patterns of Death, and no Patterns of Life — that one inverted, and just as lopsided. The finding is structural rather than incidental: across nearly every pair, one pole is written and the other is a name pointing at nothing.

It matters because of what the compass actually asks. *Does this make life a little more like Heaven, or a little more like Hell, for all current and future generations?* That is a question with two poles, and a person can only answer it if both have been *shown* rather than asserted. A language that describes the good in detail and the bad in a word does not equip anyone to recognise the bad when it arrives wearing ordinary clothes ([[The compass question]], [[Hell On Earth]], [[Heaven On Earth]], [[Discernment]]).

It is also a real question and not an oversight, which is why it is a doctrine rather than a task. Writing the dark poles well is dangerous work: done badly it produces fascination, despair, or a vocabulary for accusing people. Done well it produces recognition — the ability to see a pattern early, name it plainly, and turn. The canon's existing dark writing is its best evidence that this can be done: the anti-patterns are named precisely, without heat, as diseases rather than as enemies ([[The Anti-Patterns]], [[Evil]], [[Distortion]], [[No False-Oppositional Framings]]).

The decision of *whether and how* belongs to a steward's voice and not to a catalogue. What the review records is only that the architecture calls for them, and that the compass cannot be read with one pole missing.
