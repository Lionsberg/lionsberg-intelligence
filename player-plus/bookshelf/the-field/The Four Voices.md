# The Four Voices

*A writing convention that keeps a scene's voices apart, so a reader always knows who is speaking without being told.*

**Plain prose** is the telling itself, in the teller's voice: the Story as it happens. **Italic passages set off by rules** are the frame — the place the telling is being told from, in close third person, which may name the teller and never explains them. **Inline italics inside the telling** are the teller turning and addressing the listeners directly — *put your right hand on your chest* — and words of power given weight. **A blockquote** is the inbreaking Voice: when the Source, the Whisper, or the Song speaks directly, held rare so that its arrival is an event ([[Story]], [[Storytelling]], [[The Divine Whisper]]).

It is load-bearing because a story that carries teaching, a frame, and a sacred register all at once will collapse into sermon unless the registers are kept visibly distinct. A reader who cannot tell the tale from the commentary stops trusting either. Typography does the work that an explanation would otherwise have to do, and does it without interrupting ([[The LIØNSBERG Voice]], [[Wise Right Speech]], [[Parable]]).

The fourth voice is the one the convention exists to protect. If the Source can speak on any page, it speaks on none of them; keeping the blockquote rare is what makes it land when it comes ([[The Silence That Speaks]], [[Reverence]], [[The Song of Creation]]).

And the frame voice earns its own register because it does something no preface could: it seats the reader in the circle, so that the telling's *we* is legitimate — teller and listeners together — without ever becoming a sermon's *you* ([[Belonging]], [[Share the Stories]], [[The Great Story]]).
