# Idolatry (of the word and of the doctrine)

*Mistaking the map for the terrain, the symbol for the reality, the word for the Word — and the doctrines and propositions are idols as surely as the statues were.*

The old warnings were about objects: something made of gold or stone, set up, bowed to. The canon's extension is the useful part. A doctrine, a theology, a carefully worded proposition, a framework — each is also a human attempt to get the ineffable into a manageable container, and each falls just as short. A thousand written statements about a person do not capture that person; words about the Source are pointers, sacred tools, icons that guide toward the Reality and are not it.

So idolatry, precisely stated, is a **misplaced focus on the medium**. Not the use of words — the canon uses many — but the moment the medium stops being a means and becomes the end. The tell is what a person defends. When the wording is defended rather than the Reality it pointed at, when a revision of the formula is felt as an attack on the sacred, the exchange has already happened.

The antidote the canon names is not better wording. It is direct relationship — to seek and worship in Spirit and in truth, so that the doctrine has something to be checked against ([[Gnosis]], [[Types Of Knowing]], [[Receiving Transmission]]). Beside it stands the standing discipline of vigilance: make sure your word tools, doctrines, and rituals keep serving as means ([[Word Tools]], [[Do Not Concretize the Absolute]], [[Everything Is Current Best Understanding]]).

This is the failure at the scale of an idea. The canon names two neighbours at other scales: [[Institutional Idolatry]], where a vessel's survival becomes the goal, and [[The Golden Calf]], where a made thing is put at the centre and worshipped. All three are the same exchange — something fashioned taking the place of something given — and the reason this one is listed first is that it is the least visible. A person can refuse every statue and still have quietly made an idol of their own sentence.
