# The interoperability test

*Another Cell, given only your files, can say who you are, what you promised whom by when, what you did and will do, what you have seen on the road, and how to reach you — and can hand you a gift, a request, or a Flame and see it land.*

The [[Minimum standards of interoperability|standards]] are a list of things to carry. This is the one question that tells you whether carrying them worked. It is deliberately put in the second person and the hands of a stranger: not *have we filled in the forms* but *could someone who has never met us, holding nothing but what we leave behind, understand us and act with us*.

It is load-bearing because it is a test rather than an inspection. Nobody administers it and nobody can fail you. Any Cell can run it on itself in an afternoon by handing its folder to a neighbouring Cell and asking the six questions aloud; where the neighbour hesitates, that is the standard not yet met, and the hesitation names the next thing to build. A body that can pass it can federate with anyone; a body that cannot still runs perfectly well alone, and knows exactly what it would need to do to stop being alone ([[Federation]], [[Subsidiarity]]).

It is also a guard against a particular kind of self-deception. A body always knows its own work; the question is whether the knowledge lives anywhere but in the heads of the people who did it. Most groups that believe themselves organised discover under this test that their commitments were remembered rather than recorded, that their door is a person's phone number, and that their Quest exists as an understanding nobody has written down. None of that is a moral failing. It is simply what invisible looks like from outside ([[The Record]], [[An address]]).

The test's last clause matters as much as the first. *Can hand you a gift, a request, or a Flame and see it land* — reading is only half of interoperating. A body that can be read but cannot be reached, given to, corrected, or joined has published itself without opening itself.
